# Stories and incidents — what happened, with the math

Famous runs, heists, collapses and cons from the public record, each written up with the arithmetic that decided it. Summaries are the articles' own; sources are listed on each page.

## [The Stake hack: $41 million in nine hours, and the vault that provably fair does not cover](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/the-stake-hack-41-million-in-nine-hours-and-the-vault-provably-fair-does-not-cover/)

On 4 September 2023 the largest crypto casino in the world started paying out to someone who had not won. About $41 million left its Ethereum, BNB Chain and Polygon hot wallets in minutes; services were back the same evening; days later the FBI named North Korea’s Lazarus Group. The games were never touched. That is the point.

## [The statistician who read scratch tickets without scratching them: Mohan Srivastava and the singleton flaw](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/mohan-srivastava-the-statistician-who-read-scratch-tickets-without-scratching-them/)

In 2003 a Toronto geostatistician won $3 on a gift scratch ticket, looked at the 72 numbers printed on its face and worked out which unscratched tickets were winners about nine times in ten. He priced the trick at $600 a day, decided it was not worth his time, and mailed the lottery a sorted stack of tickets instead.

## [Uston v. Resorts: the 1982 ruling that a casino cannot bar a card counter](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/uston-v-resorts-the-1982-ruling-that-a-casino-cannot-bar-a-card-counter/)

On 30 January 1979 Atlantic City’s only casino barred the best-known card counter in America from its blackjack tables. Three years and three courts later the New Jersey Supreme Court told it that the rules of the game belonged to the state, not the house, and that a player who followed them could not be shown the door for being good at them. The house did not lose the war. It got the rules changed instead.

## [Tommy Carmichael and the light wand: what a slot sensor could not see](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/tommy-carmichael-and-the-light-wand-what-a-slot-sensor-could-not-see/)

For two decades one man kept beating slot machines with tools he built at a workbench: a bent rod, a piece of piano wire, and finally a little light that blinded the machine while it paid him. Every device attacked the same thing, the gap between what a machine decided and what it could see. It is the cleanest illustration of why a payout that is arithmetic, not a coin count, has nothing for a wand to blind.

## [The Ritz laser scanner: £1.3 million in two nights, and why it was not a crime](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/the-ritz-laser-scanner-and-the-1-3-million-that-was-not-a-crime/)

In March 2004 three people walked out of the Ritz Club in London with £1.3 million from roulette, were arrested, and nine months later were told to keep the money. They had not touched the wheel. They had measured it. The case is the cleanest statement of where prediction ends and cheating begins, and of what a physical wheel gives away that a hashed one does not.

## [Wheel bias: the two men who beat roulette by writing numbers down, and why a court said it was legal](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/wheel-bias-jagger-and-garcia-pelayo/)

Roulette is unbeatable on paper because every pocket is equally likely. On a physical wheel that is an assumption, and twice in the game’s history someone tested it with a notebook, found it false, and took the casino for a fortune. One of them ended up in a supreme court. The court sided with him.

## [Edge sorting: how Phil Ivey read the backs of the cards, and why two courts called it cheating](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/phil-ivey-edge-sorting/)

In 2012 the best-known poker player alive won £7.7 million at a London casino and $9.6 million in Atlantic City without touching a card. The trick was a printing flaw on the card backs and a dealer who agreed to turn them. Both casinos refused to pay, both went to court, and the highest court in England used the case to rewrite the definition of dishonesty.

## [The superuser: when the house could see your cards](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/the-superuser-scandal/)

In 2007 an account on Absolute Poker won a tournament playing as if every rival’s hand were face up — because to him, it was. The scandal that followed, and its larger sequel at UltimateBet, showed exactly what a fairness proof does not cover.

## [The auditor was the attack: Ronald Harris and the slot machine he was paid to check](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/ronald-harris-slot-machine-scandal/)

In the 1990s the honesty of a slot machine rested on one thing — a regulator’s technician reading its source code. One of those technicians was writing backdoors into the machines he cleared, and then did something worse: he predicted a keno draw without touching the machine at all.

## [The night black came up twenty-six times: Monte Carlo, 1913](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/the-monte-carlo-black-streak/)

The most famous streak in gambling history is a story about arithmetic, not luck. The wheel did nothing unusual. The room did — and the room lost, in the single most expensive demonstration of the gambler’s fallacy ever recorded.

## [The students who beat the house: how the MIT Blackjack Team ran counting as a business](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/mit-blackjack-team/)

For two decades, teams built around MIT students carried a real mathematical edge into casinos — with spotters, signals, investor capital and audited books. What actually worked, what ended it, and why the trick is structurally impossible here.

## [The Run: how Archie Karas turned $50 into $40 million — and why he had to lose it](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/archie-karas-the-run/)

Between 1992 and 1995, a Greek-American gambler ran fifty dollars into a reported forty million, then gave every cent back. The greatest heater in gambling history is also its cleanest math lesson.

## [Japan’s four public races: how the state runs the only legal betting](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/japans-four-public-races-how-the-state-runs-the-only-legal-betting/)

Gambling is a crime in Japan under the Penal Code, and yet more than eight trillion yen a year is wagered legally on horses, bicycles, motorboats and motorcycles. The trick is four special laws, one betting system, and a payout fixed by statute at about three quarters of the pool.

## [Takarakuji: Japan’s lottery, from temple tomikuji to a billion yen](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/takarakuji-japans-lottery-from-temple-tomikuji-to-one-billion-yen/)

A monk with an awl, a wooden box of numbered tags, and a crowd outside a Tokyo temple: that was the lottery in 1800. Today it is a ¥300 ticket with a one-billion-yen top prize and a return that the law caps below half. The lottery is the one game of chance the Japanese state never stopped selling, and its numbers say something the games on this site cannot.

## [Mahjong in Japan: the 1924 boom and a century in the grey zone](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/mahjong-in-japan-the-1924-boom-and-a-century-in-the-grey-zone/)

A novelist brought it back from Manchuria, another novelist made it fashionable, and by the 1970s there were tens of thousands of parlours where the tiles were legal and the money on the table was not quite. Mahjong is the most played gambling game in Japan that is not, officially, gambling. Here is how it got there and what the line it sits on actually says.

## [Ban-sugoroku: the dice game Japan banned in 689, and the master who said play not to lose](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/ban-sugoroku-the-first-gambling-ban/)

Japan’s oldest recorded gambling law is a single line in a court chronicle, and the game it names was a cousin of backgammon that emperors played, monks lost fortunes at, and a fourteenth-century essayist turned into the best one-sentence bankroll advice ever written. The game itself is extinct. The sentence is not.

## [Chō or han: the fairest game Japan ever ran](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/cho-han-the-edo-coin-flip/)

Two dice under a bamboo cup, one question — even or odd. Chō-han offered odds no casino would ever print: exactly fifty-fifty, paid at even money. The house made its living anyway, and how it did tells you more about gambling economics than the game itself.

## [Pachinko: how Japan built a gambling industry in a country where gambling is illegal](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/pachinko-and-the-three-shop-system/)

Japan’s criminal code prohibits gambling. Japan also has tens of thousands of parlours full of machines people feed money into for hours. The bridge between those two facts is a piece of legal architecture so elegant it has survived for decades: you never win money, you win a prize — and someone else buys the prize.

## [Oicho-Kabu: the card game that gave the yakuza their name](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/oicho-kabu-and-the-word-yakuza/)

Eight-nine-three is the worst hand in a four-hundred-year-old Japanese card game: three cards that sum to twenty and score exactly zero. Read the digits aloud in the old counting — ya-ku-za — and you have the most widely accepted origin of the word.

## [Omikuji: the shrine lottery that predates every casino](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/omikuji-the-shrine-lottery/)

Japan has drawn sacred lots for over a thousand years — to read fortunes, settle disputes, and once, to pick a shōgun. The history of omikuji, and how a scratch game borrows it honestly.

## [Before Mario: Nintendo spent 66 years as a gambling-card company](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/hanafuda-and-nintendo/)

Nintendo was founded in 1889 to print hanafuda — the flower cards born from Japan’s repeated bans on gambling decks. The story of the cards, the parlors that bought them, and the pivot that followed.

## [Chinchiro: the real rules of Japan’s street dice game — with exact payouts](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/chinchiro-rules-and-odds/)

Three dice, a bowl, and a hand ranking Kaiji got mostly right. The rules of chinchirorin, the street culture around it, and the exact payout table from a real engine.
