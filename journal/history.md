## [Japan’s four public races: how the state runs the only legal betting](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/japans-four-public-races-how-the-state-runs-the-only-legal-betting/)

_2026-09-13 · 9 min_

Japan’s Penal Code makes gambling a crime, and the only bets that escape it are the ones a specific statute permits. For racing there are four: **horse racing** (betting legalised in **1923**, the present Horse Racing Act of **1948**, the Japan Racing Association from **1954**), **keirin** bicycle racing (Bicycle Racing Act, **1948**; first meeting at Kokura that November), **auto race** motorcycle speedway (Auto Race Act, **1950**; first meeting at Funabashi), and **kyōtei** motorboat racing (Motorboat Racing Act, **1951**; racing from **1952**). Together they are the **kōei kyōgi**, the public races, run by public bodies for public revenue. All four use **pari-mutuel** betting: every wager goes into one pool, the operator keeps a share fixed by law, and the rest is divided among the winning tickets in proportion to what they staked. That share is the whole economics of the system. For horse racing the law returns **75%** of sales to bettors and keeps 25%; the other three sit in the same band, between roughly 70 and 80 percent. On that footing the JRA alone took about **¥3.36 trillion** in 2024 and the four sports together around **¥8.5 trillion**. For comparison, the originals on this site return between about **96.5%** (bingo) and **99.07%** (the best keno table), figures read from the engine rather than set by a ministry.

## [Takarakuji: Japan’s lottery, from temple tomikuji to a billion yen](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/takarakuji-japans-lottery-from-temple-tomikuji-to-one-billion-yen/)

_2026-09-11 · 8 min_

**Takarakuji** (宝くじ) is Japan’s public lottery, run under a 1948 law that lets prefectures and designated cities issue tickets and keeps a share of the sales for local government. Its ancestor is **tomikuji** (富籤), the temple lottery of the Edo period: numbered wooden tags were shaken in a box and pierced with an awl, with the earliest recorded drawing at **Ryūanji temple** near Osaka in the late sixteenth century and the great Edo drawings at Yanaka, Meguro and Yushima. The shogunate banned tomikuji in **1842** in the Tenpō reforms. The modern lottery began in **1945**, first as a wartime issue and then, from October, as the government takarakuji at **¥10 a ticket**. Today the **Year-end Jumbo** pays **¥700 million** for first prize plus **¥150 million** for each adjacent number, a billion yen in a row of three tickets, at odds of about **one in 20 million** per ticket. By law the prize pool cannot exceed **50%** of sales, and in practice the return is around **46%**. For comparison, the keno tables on this site return between 98.65% and 99.07% and the bingo card about 96.5%, figures computed from the engine rather than published as a promise.

## [Mahjong in Japan: the 1924 boom and a century in the grey zone](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/mahjong-in-japan-the-1924-boom-and-a-century-in-the-grey-zone/)

_2026-09-11 · 8 min_

**Mahjong** reached Japan from China in the first years of the twentieth century; the novelist **Natsume Sōseki** described it in 1909 after seeing it played in Manchuria. The boom came in the 1920s, when the writer and publisher **Kikuchi Kan** championed the game, the first parlours opened in Tokyo around **1924**, and a national federation was founded in 1929. The postwar decades made it a mass pastime, played with the **riichi** rules that the world now calls Japanese mahjong, and by the 1970s the number of parlours ran into the tens of thousands. Its legal position has not changed in a century. Article 185 of the Penal Code punishes gambling but exempts stakes that are **“a thing provided for momentary amusement”**, which courts have held does not cover cash; parlours are licensed as amusement businesses that rent tables and are not permitted to host betting. In practice small-stake games are tolerated and occasionally prosecuted, most visibly in **2020**, when Tokyo’s chief prosecutor resigned over mahjong for money with reporters and was later fined. It is a grey zone by design, and it is the opposite of a published edge: on this site every game states its return and is either offered or not.

## [Ban-sugoroku: the dice game Japan banned in 689, and the master who said play not to lose](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/ban-sugoroku-the-first-gambling-ban/)

_2026-09-08 · 8 min_

**Ban-sugoroku** (盤双六, “board double-six”) was a two-player race game of the backgammon family: fifteen pieces a side, two dice, and a board of twelve points per half, brought to Japan from the continent by the seventh century. In the twelfth month of **689** the court chronicle Nihon Shoki records that **Empress Jitō prohibited sugoroku**, the first gambling ban in Japanese written history, a few years after the same chronicle shows her predecessor Tenmu playing dice games with his courtiers. The game survived the ban by a thousand years: Heian courtiers wagered on it, Emperor Shirakawa listed **“the dice of sugoroku”** among the three things he could not control, and around 1330 the essayist Kenkō recorded a sugoroku master’s rule, **“do not play to win; play not to lose”**. Ban-sugoroku died out in the Edo period, replaced by the picture race game e-sugoroku that families still play at New Year. On this site the dice it was built on are drawn one uniform number at a time from a committed seed, which is the one thing Shirakawa could not have had.

## [Chō or han: the fairest game Japan ever ran](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/cho-han-the-edo-coin-flip/)

_2026-08-31 · 9 min_

**Chō-han (丁半)** is the dice game of Edo-period Japan: a dealer shakes two dice in a cup, slams it down, and players bet **chō** (even sum) or **han** (odd). Across the 36 ways two dice can land, exactly **18 are even and 18 are odd** — a true fifty-fifty paid at even money, a game with **no house edge in its odds at all**. The bakuto gambling clans who ran it profited instead by taking an open cut of winning bets. Modern casinos fold that price into the odds; chō-han charged it at the door, and the difference between the two models is the real subject of this article.

## [Pachinko: how Japan built a gambling industry in a country where gambling is illegal](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/pachinko-and-the-three-shop-system/)

_2026-08-07 · 8 min_

Pachinko is legal because, on paper, it is not gambling: a player wins **balls**, exchanges them for **prizes** inside the parlour, and — if they want cash — sells those prizes at a **legally separate business off the premises**. This is the **three-shop system** (三店方式): parlour, prize counter and buyer are formally unrelated, so no single entity pays out money for a game of chance. The structure is decades old, openly known, and has made pachinko one of the largest leisure industries in the world.

## [Oicho-Kabu: the card game that gave the yakuza their name](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/oicho-kabu-and-the-word-yakuza/)

_2026-08-07 · 7 min_

Oicho-Kabu is a Japanese card game played to a score of **nine**, where only the last digit of a sum counts. The hand **8-9-3** totals twenty and therefore scores **zero** — worthless. Spoken in the older readings of the numerals it is **ya-ku-za**, and that is the accepted origin of the word: a self-description by gamblers as the useless hand, later attached to the organisations that grew out of the gambling houses.

## [Omikuji: the shrine lottery that predates every casino](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/omikuji-the-shrine-lottery/)

_2026-07-24 · 6 min_

**Omikuji** (御神籤) are the fortune lots drawn at Japanese shrines and temples: shake a box, draw a stick, receive a slip ranked from **大吉** (great blessing) down to **凶** (curse). The numbered-lot-with-poem tradition is attributed to the 10th-century monk **Ryōgen**, and lot-drawing itself was once serious statecraft — in 1428 Japan chose a shōgun by drawing lots.

## [Before Mario: Nintendo spent 66 years as a gambling-card company](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/hanafuda-and-nintendo/)

_2026-07-24 · 7 min_

**Nintendo** was founded in Kyoto in **1889** by Fusajiro Yamauchi as a workshop printing **hanafuda** (花札, “flower cards”) — a 48-card deck that evolved specifically to dodge Edo-era gambling bans by replacing numbers with flowers. Nintendo sold playing cards for 66 years before its first toy, and it still sells hanafuda today.

## [Chinchiro: the real rules of Japan’s street dice game — with exact payouts](https://betkyo.com/en/blog/chinchiro-rules-and-odds/)

_2026-07-24 · 9 min_

**Chinchirorin** is a Japanese three-dice game rolled into a bowl: triples and 4·5·6 win instantly, 1·2·3 loses, and a pair turns the odd die into your **point**. The dealer rolls first and wins ties — that single rule is where the house lives. In Betkyo’s engine the best possible return is **×6**, for rolling 1·1·1 against a dealer point.
